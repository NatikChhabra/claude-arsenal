# beatsheet <topic>

status: beta

Comedy = planned bits + his improv. A video built only on improv felt flat to its own creator: all seasoning, no meal. So every video stacks seven layers: progress you can see, a local frame, WRITTEN
BITS, one running gag (3 beats + a payoff), his live reactions, bridges (his live lines or a voice-over written after
recording), the edit layer. Written lines are welcome, but as separate drop-in parts: a modular pack he picks from, rewrites or skips, never a script read straight through.
The pack layout: `references/pack-template.md`.

## 0. Context first
Read the topic's primer (screen map, mechanics, jargon, where the comedy is); write it first if missing (from the topic
itself and a web search, never guessed). Research the live context (news, collabs, trends, any creator the video
touches). Check the publisher's video policy for a game. Never ask him what research or the recording answers.

## Before writing
- Every item on the map exists in the topic (no invented features); check anything the primer doesn't cover.
- **Title** = the topic's own situation, first person, true just by playing ("I Became a Chef"), in the channel template
  (skill `yt-package`). **Click test:** title + thumbnail explain the video in ONE sentence, and you'd click it from a
  creator you've never heard of. Two sentences = simplify the premise. Never a challenge he has to perform.
- **Golden rule:** would this pull the people who watched the last 3 videos? If not, it is a deliberate new block (a 4-8
  video experiment), never a one-off in the middle of the lineup.
- **Escalation to watch for:** one line on where the topic tops its own premise later (a bigger version the topic itself
  throws at him), so the edit has a step-further moment to tease and pay off. Never a self-imposed rule or fake stakes.
- **Demand slot:** say whether this is the demand-slot video (skill `topic-pick`).
- **Long topic = 2 episodes:** if one session holds more than one video, plan Part 1 + a banked Part 2 and mark where
  Part 1 ends (a cliffhanger the topic gives, never staged). Day-N sagas are the exception: standalone films, each its
  own title and payoff, never "Part N" (a Part 2 drew 0.36x of its Part 1 in one language niche).
- Check older packs for this topic so nothing repeats; carry over what viewers liked (callbacks).

## Output: TWO files he reads, nothing else
1. **STORY SCRIPT** (`<date>_<topic>_story.md`): the question, his arc, the scenes he plays to (choices he makes on
   camera so the story really happens), two honest lines, the ending, optional lines. He reads it before recording.
2. **BIT PACK** (`<date>_<topic>.md`): only the bits, grouped by act, stop points and the one trap in the act headings.
   No spares, no "also read" (a creator deleted everything else in the sheet and said "only one bit pack. Nothing else").
Everything else goes in a hidden plan file the edit reads (story map, live rules, extras, editor notes). Links to the
other files at the top of each.

## Rules
- **Bits:** short, in his language mix and lowercase dry voice, tied to a moment the topic WILL produce, numbers checked
  against the primer. Each uses one device: a saying or rule taken literally, a logical escalation that flips, a real
  person's exact words as the punch, an undercut of a known image, understatement, or self-deprecation. Never a stock
  simile template ("<thing> = <national exam / commuter train / wedding>"), never "just like me", never "every X has a Y".
  Craft from English sketch writers: a familiar format with one variable flipped, era contrast, POV commitment, one
  premise escalated deadpan. Max 3 parent bits. 12 strong beat 20 mixed. Never sex, race, religion, politics, tragedies or roasting real people
  unless he says so. Bar: the comedians he names as role models.
- **Sounds like him:** nouns and topic words in the language he uses for them, his particles and slang, never bookish
  words. If the project has a speech checker, run it (zero bookish words, the pack's language mix near his measured
  share); otherwise compare every line against 20 of his real transcript lines and cut what he would never say.
- **Source tags (hard gate):** every bit and voice-over line carries its source in an HTML comment (so a notes app shows
  it clean): `<!-- [src: <transcript> <mm:ss> | his words as transcribed] -->` for the moment it borrows,
  `<!-- [src: topic <primer path>] -->` for the fact it rests on, or `<!-- [src: story <what it bends>] -->` for creative
  license. A transcript tag whose words aren't said within 20 s of that time is invalid (a garbled transcript line is no
  proof). No source = it doesn't ship. A voice-over line needs a transcript tag (it can only borrow what he said).
- **Blind-tested before he sees them:** 3 fresh judges score the bits against a benchmark of real comedians' lines (a
  first round scored written bits 4.7 vs 6.5 for real comedians, and 43% read as machine-written). Bits averaging >= 5
  make the pack. Judges RANK; they never cut a bit he picked or praised (those stay in the one pack).
- **Creative license, two lines held:** the story, bits and voice-over may bend or invent motives, backstory and topic
  facts; held: the day count and money numbers shown or said are real (a fake "100 days" is what comments catch), and
  nothing clashes with the picture.
- **The voice-over script is written AFTER recording, never here.** The pack's live rules make him say the raw material
  aloud while playing (the plan before a big choice, the number at the start of each day, what he expects), because a script line
  can only borrow what he said or what the screen showed. Budget a ~30-45 min watch-back voice-over session in the plan.
- No challenges, no self-imposed rules, no fake stakes, no stock local cliches.
- Keep the plan's one-screen summary one screen tall; cut the least interesting items if it sprawls.
